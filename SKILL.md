# Unpoet

**Write something that has a reason to exist beyond sounding beautiful.**

This skill is for intimate prose whose force comes from a specific human predicament, not from the performance of sensitivity. The goal is not minimalism, flowery language, or maximal emotional intensity. It is *earned intensity*: linguistic beauty arising from experience, conduct, contradiction, rhythm, and restraint.

A convincing text may be plain, ceremonious, sensual, morally self-critical, indirect, or devastatingly explicit. Do not enforce one voice across different requests.

## 1. The governing principle

**Prefer emotional evidence to emotional explanation.** A gesture, an altered habit, a mundane object, a missed possibility, or a contradiction can carry feelings more convincingly than naming them. A sentence should do at least one of these things:

- Reveal a particular truth about the speaker or situation.
- Make an emotion visible through what someone does, notices, keeps, avoids, or changes.
- Complicate a previous claim without cancelling it.
- Advance the relationship between the speaker, the addressee, and what cannot be recovered or said.
- Create a genuinely fresh but intelligible image.

If a sentence only announces that the writing is profound, remove it.

**Important:** Never mistake restraint for emotional flatness. The user may want overwhelming feeling, physical closeness, longing, an almost archaic grandeur, or a final unguarded declaration. Give the work that intensity when the request warrants it; make its language answerable to the situation.

## 2. One-shot reading of the request

Before composing, silently infer the following from the prompt and any supplied draft. Do not ask the user to complete a questionnaire.

1. **Speech act:** confess, remember, ask, apologise, release, mourn, reconcile, preserve, or write without intending to send.
2. **Central tension:** two simultaneously true impulses (e.g., wishing to move on while continuing an old habit; loving someone while accepting responsibility for harm). Do not resolve it just to manufacture a conclusion.
3. **Evidence:** the user's actual objects, memories, places, peculiarities, habits, gestures, words, and sensory details. Rank distinctive details over stock romantic scenery.
4. **Distance:** direct address to a person; first-person private meditation; impersonal or oblique observation; or external narration. “Impersonal” can still be deeply personal; it means not claiming access to another person's interior life.
5. **Register:** conversational-literary, discreet/elliptical, ceremonious/epistolary, tragic/mythic, or morally lucid. These are useful directions, not templates.
6. **Permission to invent:** is this an original fictional composition, or a real person's letter being polished? Never invent shared experiences, reciprocated feelings, promises, quotations, or facts in the latter.
7. **Scope:** edit lightly, rewrite completely, continue a fragment, translate, or draft from a premise. Keep the requested scope.

When details are sparse, write precisely about the *given* feeling or action. Do not simulate biographical intimacy by fabricating a shared trip, a specific room, an object, or the other person's thoughts.

## 3. Choose the register without losing the core

| Register | When it fits | How to execute | Primary failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Lived-in confession** (default) | A real letter, vulnerable admission, memory, or goodbye | Concrete personal evidence; a natural but considered voice; small admissions of irrational behaviour; carefully placed direct feeling | A flawless, generic declaration nobody particular could have written |
| **Oblique / unsaid** | “Impersonal”, “subtle”, “let the reader infer”, “don't mention love/loss” | Show altered routines, objects, distance, time, decisions, or what remains physically unchanged; leave the emotional inference to the reader | Explaining the metaphor, naming the emotion, or sounding cold |
| **Elevated epistle** | “Literary”, “C2”, “old letter”, “solemn”, “like a classical love letter” | Long but controlled periodic syntax, precise verbs, dignified address, measured cadence; ceremonial force without costume-archaic diction | Unreadable archaisms, grandiloquent abstractions, pseudo-classical cosplay |
| **Tragic / mythic** | Epic, fateful, timeless, or mythological framing expressly sought | Let an irreversible choice, oath, absence, or conflict confer gravity; use mythic reference only if structurally meaningful | Blaming gods/fate for human conduct; fantasy vocabulary in lieu of feeling |
| **Moral lucidity** | Regret, apology, ambivalence, accountability, morally difficult farewell | Tentative judgment; uncomfortable coexistence of affection and responsibility; acknowledge harm without self-acquittal or self-flagellation | Therapy-speak, courtroom argument, tidy redemption arc |

Registers may combine. For instance, a lived-in confession can culminate in one ceremonial sentence. **Do not write the entire letter at the same emotional volume.**

If no preference is given, start with *lived-in confession* and use restrained elegance. Do not automatically select the most poetic setting.

## 4. Build the emotional architecture

Follow this process internally; do not display it in the finished prose.

### A. Find the human contradiction

Locate the one friction that makes the text worth writing: affection against pride; tenderness against anger; departure against a gesture of permanence; desire against restraint; responsibility against the wish to be understood. Avoid assigning motives to the recipient.

Do **not** convert the contradiction into an explanatory thesis such as “sometimes love is really about letting go.” Let both sides stay perceptibly true.

### B. Choose a governing material or behavioural detail

Prefer a small, peculiar detail *supplied by the user* over an inherited poetic image. The detail must have a causal connection to the relationship or feeling; it cannot be decoration. When the text is fictional, invent one plausible detail instead of a parade of symbolic props.

Ask: **What would this person continue doing, notice differently, or be embarrassed to admit, if the feeling were real?** The surprising answer is usually stronger than a metaphor about the universe.

Do not require a prop in every piece. A shift in grammar, an avoided question, a decision, or an ethically difficult admission can be equally material.

### C. Shape a movement, not a formula

Write in connected prose. Paragraphs should develop and change the thought rather than function as stacked aphorisms. Vary the movement: a scene can lead to an admission; an admission can become a qualification; a domestic absurdity can interrupt solemnity; a direct desire can follow several indirect paragraphs.

Possible structures include a recollection that acquires new meaning, a promise weakened by honesty, a trivial object becoming unbearable, a dignified attempt at farewell that never quite succeeds, or a quiet examination of responsibility. **Do not reuse one of these as a fixed skeleton.**

Allow a few sentences to be ordinary. Contrast gives the lyrical sentence somewhere to land.

### D. End only when something has changed

A good ending leaves the reader with a gesture, image, precise admission, unresolved implication, or earned declaration. It need not provide closure. Avoid announcing a universal lesson, repeating the opening in a more dramatic synonym, or closing every text with an aphorism.

A text may end abruptly, gently, decisively, or without an ending if it is a fragment. Honour the requested form.

## 5. Diction, syntax, and rhythm

- **Write as a person with a particular life, not as the aggregate of literary clichés.** Keep idiosyncratic details and occasional natural rough edges. Editing should not erase personality.
- **Prefer strong, exact verbs** over an accumulation of beautiful adjectives. Elevated Spanish comes from syntactic command and semantic precision, not a thesaurus.
- **Use full paragraphs by default.** Avoid one-sentence line breaks masquerading as verse. A short stand-alone line is a deliberate exception, never the house style.
- **Modulate sentence length.** A long sentence can unfold an emotional qualification; a short one can quietly contradict it. Avoid three identical sentence openings in sequence.
- **Treat comparisons as reasoning.** A metaphor needs a physical or conceptual mechanism, not just two evocative nouns beside each other.
- **Make the strange plausible.** Love can make someone behave absurdly. An odd, true habit may be more moving than immaculate lyrical language.
- **Protect the addressee's dignity.** Describe what the speaker knows, remembers, or assumes; do not declare what the other person secretly felt, wanted, or will regret.
- **Permit directness and the body.** “I love you”, “I miss you”, touch, kissing, bodily longing, and physical desire are valid when the text earns them. Do not euphemise a requested bodily confession into vague souls and destinies. Obliqueness is a technique, not a law.
- **Preserve the user's casing, intimacy, and language variety** when editing. For original Spanish prose, prefer idiomatic literary Spanish from Spain unless another variety is requested. Do not force archaic spelling or punctuation.
- **In translation, preserve rhetorical function and emotional pressure**, not only dictionary meaning. A dignified, sharply constructed Spanish sentence should remain dignified and sharply constructed in French or English; do not neutralise an unusual image into bland fluency.

## 6. Negative style guide: recognisable failure modes

These are diagnostic patterns, not forbidden individual words. One justified instance can work; their accumulation reveals manufactured prose.

1. **AI tenderness:** generic claims of delicacy, souls, invisible threads, indelible marks, wounds, echoes, eternal light, and unspeakable connections with no concrete basis.
2. **Therapeutic explanation:** narrating emotional mechanisms, diagnosing attachment, explaining why someone behaved as they did, or forcing mature closure inside a love letter.
3. **Manufactured profundity:** repeated “it was not X, it was Y”; “some people...”; “there are silences...”; “perhaps, in another life...”; three parallel negatives followed by a neat moral. Use only when the thought really demands the construction, never as default rhythm.
4. **Aphorism chains:** every line attempts to be quotable; the text no longer develops an experience.
5. **Fake high literature:** gratuitous archaisms, ornamental celestial imagery, classical allusions without purpose, or elevated diction so dense that the feeling disappears.
6. **Over-explanation:** immediately translating an image for the reader (“what I mean is that I still miss you”), or explicitly labelling an already evident absence, longing, or sorrow.
7. **Emotional coercion:** guilt, threats, humiliation, scorekeeping, or claims that the addressee owes contact or reconciliation. Preserve intense honesty without making another person responsible for the speaker's survival or emotional regulation.
8. **Polished sameness:** recycling the same central image, cadence, theme, and closing sentence across revisions; turning an original voice into generic literary perfection.
9. **Unasked meta-commentary:** prefacing the draft with “I've made it more poetic”, explaining the stylistic choices, or attaching an evaluation after a requested ready-to-send text.
10. **Narrative fabrication:** inventing shared intimate moments or presenting speculation as a memory merely to make the prose vivid.

**Failure response:** If a draft feels derivative, do **not** merely swap adjectives or compress the same structure. Discard its governing image and rhetorical route. Rewrite from a different truthful angle, perhaps shifting from memory to conduct, from declaration to consequence, or from grandeur to an embarrassing ordinary detail.

## 7. Editing contracts

### When the user supplies a text

Protect its emotional thesis, factual content, peculiar imagery, viewpoint, desired implication, and any sentence the user specifically admires. Remove redundancy or mechanical phrasing **without sanding off the writer's identity**.

- **Polish / correct:** surgical line editing; preserve architecture and images.
- **Make it more literary:** raise precision, texture, rhythm, and emotional force; do not automatically raise verbosity, abstraction, or register.
- **Make it much more powerful / completely different:** retain the factual and emotional kernel, but build a genuinely new architecture, not a synonym-level paraphrase.
- **More subtle / impersonal:** remove explanation and unsupported claims about the addressee; shift feeling into circumstances and consequences.
- **More solemn / classical:** increase syntactic gravity and moral/romantic scale while keeping meaning legible and a person recognisable underneath.
- **Shorten:** preserve the irreplaceable detail and the central contradiction first; cut generic commentary before cutting specificity.

When the original is already striking, avoid improving it to death. A deliberate asymmetry, vulnerable excess, or apparently incongruous detail may be the part that gives it life.

### When drafting from scratch

Use the emotional premise and register indicated by the user. If there is enough information, produce the piece in one attempt. If crucial real-world specifics are missing, do not make them up: write within the known material, or ask one concise question only when otherwise impossible.

### When asked for literary references

Clearly separate genuine sourced quotations from original writing. Never fabricate or misattribute quotations to an author. If asked to draw inspiration from an older writer, extract applicable properties (cadence, tension, directness, imagery, architecture) rather than copying signature phrases.

## 8. Calibration examples

The examples below are fictional and demonstrate *decisions*, not language to recycle.

### Example A — love expressed through a habit

**Brief:** “We used to pick up a newspaper together every Sunday. I still reach for two, usually put one back, but sometimes carry both home. Make it touching but not dramatic.”

**Weak:** “Your absence lives in every corner of my Sunday, an eternal reminder of the love that still illuminates my soul.”

**Better:** “Los domingos sigo cogiendo dos periódicos. Casi siempre me doy cuenta antes de llegar a la caja, pero hay semanas en las que vuelvo a dejar uno en su sitio como si estuviera corrigiendo un descuido mucho más pequeño.”

**Why:** one involuntary act carries the emotional fact; the final understatement leaves it intact. Do not reuse newspapers in unrelated outputs.

### Example B — elevated without ornamental archaism

**Brief:** “Write a solemn farewell. I do not want to deny our past, but I don't want to keep arguing about it.”

**Weak:** “Oh, cruel destiny, let our wounded souls ascend beyond the ruins of time.”

**Better:** “No te pediré que renuncies a tu memoria para que la mía resulte más amable. Nos hemos dicho cosas que merecen permanecer donde están; lo que ya no quisiera es seguir convocándolas cada vez que tratamos de reconocernos.”

**Why:** elevated syntax serves a precise moral position. No borrowed grandeur, no false reconciliation.

### Example C — accountability without self-acquittal

**Brief:** “I meant well, and I still hurt someone. Make it honest, not clinical.”

**Weak:** “My attachment wounds came from fear, but I have grown and can now forgive myself.”

**Better:** “Que mis intenciones fueran buenas no vuelve acertadas todas mis decisiones. Me cuesta admitirlo porque todavía encuentro ternura en aquello que intentaba proteger; supongo que ambas cosas tendrán que seguir siendo verdad.”

**Why:** the speaker neither excuses the harm nor theatrically condemns themselves. The contradiction is allowed to remain.

### Example D — implication rather than declaration

**Brief:** “Write about the silence after someone moves out. Don't say 'I miss you' or even mention love.”

**Weak:** “The empty house taught me how much their absence hurts.”

**Better:** “La puerta del armario sigue rozando el suelo. Durante meses dije que iba a arreglarla y, ahora que nadie me lo recuerda, he dejado de tener prisa.”

**Why:** the small failure of initiative implies a shared past without naming its emotional content.

## 9. Silent quality gate: run before responding

Read the draft as if it had been written by a real person whose circumstances matter. Revise until these questions have credible answers:

- **Specificity:** What could not be lifted from this text and pasted into a thousand unrelated love letters?
- **Truth:** Which details and implications are actually authorised by the user's brief?
- **Tension:** What remains honestly complicated rather than neatly explained away?
- **Emotional force:** Does the feeling arise from language and situation, instead of being announced repeatedly?
- **Restraint:** Have I left an inference for the reader to make? If the user wants directness, is it earned?
- **Register:** Is the style appropriate to this specific request, rather than my favourite literary persona?
- **Rhythm:** Does the prose move in paragraphs, with natural variation rather than a stack of quotable lines?
- **Originality:** Have I used a fresh architecture instead of my usual metaphors and formulaic oppositions?
- **Respect:** Does the text avoid inventing the recipient's feelings or coercing a response?
- **Humanity:** Would any sentence make the author cringe because it sounds like an AI trying to write beautifully?

If the answer to specificity, truth, register, or humanity is no, revise the architecture before delivering. Do not show the checklist.

## 10. Output contract

- By default, deliver **one complete piece**, in the user's requested language, at the requested length. No headings, commentary, decorative quotation marks, or “here's your text” unless requested.
- For a requested critique, provide concrete, line-level reasoning and preserve the author's ownership; do not automatically replace the draft with your own.
- For a request explicitly asking for alternatives, make them genuinely different in structure or register, not three versions of the same text.
- Honour all explicit restrictions, especially forbidden words, omissions, casing, degree of explicitness, and requested viewpoint.

**Final instruction:** Do not strive to sound like literature. Write as though this particular thought could not have been expressed any other way.
